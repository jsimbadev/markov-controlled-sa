Stationary calibration and Markov augmentation for Adam-type adaptive MCMC

Author
Affiliation

Jordan Simbananiye

University College London (UCL) - CCMI CDT

Published

18 November 2026

Abstract

Adam-type adaptation replaces an ordinary stochastic-approximation innovation by a normalised function of fixed-gain first- and second-moment memories. We identify the stationary field under Markov innovations and its covariance correction at Robbins–Monro roots. A finite-dimensional Markov augmentation admits a stationary law under moment assumptions. Under stronger coupling and regularity conditions, geometric Wasserstein contraction of the driver lifts to the augmented process. We derive one-step parameter perturbation bounds, invariant-law regularity and a restricted Lipschitz-space Poisson estimate, distinguishing the latter’s additional operator assumption. The resulting estimates specify the ingredients for a controlled-Markov ODE approximation; no adaptive ODE-tracking theorem is asserted.

1 Introduction

Adaptive MCMC commonly calibrates a sampling-kernel parameter by Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation [1, 2]. For example, a proposal scale can be adjusted using an acceptance-rate residual relative to a prescribed target. With the parameter frozen at \(\theta\), the stationary expectation of that residual defines a field \(h(\theta)\); the intended calibration equation is \(h(\theta)=0\). More generally, adaptation seeks a root of a prescribed stationary mean-field equation.

Adam replaces the ordinary innovation by a nonlinear function of exponentially weighted first- and second-moment estimates. The memory coefficients are fixed, while the parameter gain decreases. Linear momentum preserves the frozen mean field in expectation, but Adam’s random normaliser is coupled to its numerator. Its stationary field \(F(\theta)\) need not coincide with \(h(\theta)\), and the two calibration equations need not have the same roots.

Identifying this stationary field, proving that the actual stochastic recursion tracks an ODE, and proving convergence to a particular equilibrium are distinct questions. An ODE approximation with field \(F\) does not establish convergence to a root of \(h\), or even convergence to a single equilibrium of \(F\). Dereich–Jentzen–Kassing [3, Definition 1.3 and Theorem 1.5] identify the Adam field and establish an ODE approximation under IID innovations, with moment, regularity and gain assumptions and \(\alpha^2<\beta<1\). Their analysis provides the IID benchmark; it does not supply the corresponding result for parameter-controlled Markov observations.

In adaptive MCMC the innovations are dependent and their transition law changes with the adaptation parameter. Freezing that parameter gives a homogeneous Markov driver. This note identifies the stationary calibration issue for that frozen system and constructs its stationary Adam memories. The corresponding finite-dimensional Markov augmentation identifies the stationary memory law. We then use a forward coupling argument to obtain quantitative Wasserstein contraction of the augmented kernel, without requiring a reversed driving chain.

We then consider the stronger quantitative structure needed for stochastic approximation. A geometric coupling estimate for the driver, together with contractive memory and regularity of its forcing, yields Wasserstein contraction of the augmented kernel. This permits the perturbation theory of [4, Theorem 3.1] to be applied to the parameter-indexed invariant laws, and the Poisson theory of [5] to be applied to Lipschitz observables. The assumptions are stated at the level of the driver and the memory update. The resulting stationary and parameter-regularity estimates are separate from the final adaptive ODE approximation, for which additional Poisson and stochastic-approximation conditions are identified but not inferred merely from frozen ergodicity.

The covariance identity remains the calibration question motivating these constructions. We distinguish the weaker assumptions sufficient to define that covariance from the additional contraction and parameter-regularity assumptions used to study the controlled recursion.

2 Stationary calibration and the Adam mean field

2.1 Frozen kernels and probability laws

Let \((\mathsf X,\mathcal A)\) be a standard Borel measurable space and \(\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d\) a Borel parameter set. For each \(\theta\in\Theta\), let \(P_\theta\) be a Markov transition kernel on \(\mathsf X\), with \((\theta,x)\mapsto P_\theta(x,A)\) measurable for every \(A\in\mathcal A\). Let \(H:\Theta\times\mathsf X\to\mathbb{R}^d\) be measurable and write \(H_\theta(x)=H(\theta,x)\). Write \(\mu(f)=\int f\,d\mu\) when this integral exists. The action of a kernel on a probability measure is

\[ (\mu P_\theta)(A)=\int_{\mathsf X}P_\theta(x,A)\,\mu(dx),\qquad A\in\mathcal A. \tag{2.1}\]

An invariant probability \(\pi_\theta\) satisfies

\[ \pi_\theta P_\theta=\pi_\theta. \tag{2.2}\]

Thus one transition preserves the marginal \(\pi_\theta\) when the parameter is fixed. In the usual MCMC setting the sampling kernels share a target \(\pi_\theta=\pi\), although their transition dependence changes with \(\theta\).

Assumption 2.1 (Frozen driver and innovation moment) For each \(\theta\in\Theta\), \(P_\theta\) has a unique invariant probability \(\pi_\theta\), and

\[ \pi_\theta(\|H_\theta\|^2)<\infty. \]

All subsequent stationary constructions are pointwise in \(\theta\); no regularity of the family of invariant laws is assumed here. Under Assumption 2.1, the consistent stationary finite-dimensional laws specified by \(\pi_\theta\) and \(P_\theta\) extend, by the Kolmogorov extension theorem, to a two-sided stationary Markov chain \((X_k^\theta)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\) on this standard Borel space. Denote its law on \((\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal A^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}})\) by \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\). Its one-time marginal is \(\pi_\theta\), and its forward transition conditional on the entire past is \(P_\theta\). The marginal \(\pi_\theta\) describes one observation; \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\) also describes temporal dependence and is generally not a product law.

The ordinary frozen mean field is

\[ h(\theta)=\pi_\theta(H_\theta) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}[H_\theta(X_0^\theta)]. \tag{2.3}\]

A Robbins–Monro root \(\theta^\star\) satisfies \(h(\theta^\star)=0\). For acceptance-rate calibration one may use \(H_\theta(x)=a_\theta(x)-a^\star\), where \(a_\theta(x)\) is the conditional expected acceptance probability and \(a^\star\) is the target rate. If the observed residual instead uses proposals or acceptance indicators, the driving state must include the variables needed to make that observation a function of its current state; the invariant law in Equation 2.3 then refers to that enlarged driver.

2.2 Fixed-gain memories and the stationary field

For context, a controlled recursion generates observations according to

\[ X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n\sim P_{\theta_n}(X_n,\cdot), \tag{2.4}\]

with \(\theta_n,X_n\) measurable with respect to \(\mathcal F_n\). Ordinary Robbins–Monro adaptation takes the form

\[ \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1}H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1}). \tag{2.5}\]

Here \((\gamma_n)\) are positive deterministic parameter gains. An Adam-type alternative fixes \(0\leq\alpha,\beta<1\) and \(\varepsilon>0\) and uses

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{n+1}&=\alpha M_n+(1-\alpha)H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1}),\\ V_{n+1}&=\beta V_n+(1-\beta)H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1})^{\odot2},\\ \theta_{n+1}&=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1} \frac{M_{n+1}}{\sqrt{V_{n+1}}+\varepsilon}. \end{aligned} \tag{2.6}\]

Initially \(M_0\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(V_0\in[0,\infty)^d\). Squares, square roots and division are componentwise, and \(\varepsilon\) is added in each coordinate. We use uncorrected memories, without finite-time bias factors. The gains \(1-\alpha\) and \(1-\beta\) remain fixed. The displayed recursions motivate the frozen construction; no tracking assertion is made about their changing-parameter law.

Fix \(\theta\) and set \(U_k^\theta=H_\theta(X_k^\theta)\). Define the stationary memories at time zero by

\[ M^\theta=(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=0}^\infty\alpha^j U_{-j}^\theta,\qquad V^\theta=(1-\beta)\sum_{j=0}^\infty\beta^j(U_{-j}^\theta)^{\odot2}. \tag{2.7}\]

For a zero coefficient the corresponding series consists only of its \(j=0\) term. These variables are defined under \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\).

Lemma 2.1 (Integrable stationary memories) Under Assumption 2.1, the series in Equation 2.7 converge absolutely almost surely and in \(L^1\). Moreover, \(M^\theta\in L^2\).

Proof. Put \(c_1=\pi_\theta(\|H_\theta\|)<\infty\) and \(c_2=\pi_\theta(\|H_\theta\|^2)<\infty\). Tonelli’s theorem and stationarity give

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\geq0}\alpha^j\|U_{-j}^\theta\|\right]&=c_1,\\ \mathbb{E}\left[(1-\beta)\sum_{j\geq0}\beta^j\|U_{-j}^\theta\|^2\right]&=c_2. \end{aligned} \]

Both nonnegative series are therefore finite almost surely. Since \(\|u^{\odot2}\|\leq\|u\|^2\), the expected norm tails after \(j=J\) are at most \(c_1\alpha^{J+1}\) and \(c_2\beta^{J+1}\), respectively. This also proves \(L^1\) convergence. Jensen’s inequality for the weights \((1-\alpha)\alpha^j\), whose sum is one, yields

\[ \|M^\theta\|^2\leq(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\geq0}\alpha^j\|U_{-j}^\theta\|^2. \]

Its expectation is at most \(c_2\), proving the final assertion.

The stationary Adam field is

\[ F(\theta) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}} \left[\frac{M^\theta}{\sqrt{V^\theta}+\varepsilon}\right]. \tag{2.8}\]

It is finite because \(\|M^\theta/(\sqrt{V^\theta}+\varepsilon)\|\leq\|M^\theta\|/\varepsilon\). This expectation is under the stationary frozen Markov path law, not the nonstationary law of Equation 2.4–Equation 2.6. For the IID kernel \(P_\theta(x,\cdot)=\pi_\theta(\cdot)\) it reduces to the history field of [3, Definition 1.3]. Neither the field’s existence nor the memory construction requires the additional restriction \(\alpha^2<\beta\) used in their ODE theorem.

2.3 Calibration under linear and nonlinear memory

Linear EMA momentum preserves the ordinary field. By the absolute integrability just proved, expectation and summation can be interchanged:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}[M^\theta] =(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\geq0}\alpha^j\mathbb{E}[U_{-j}^\theta] =h(\theta). \tag{2.9}\]

No independence is used. Adam instead multiplies each momentum coordinate by \(W_i^\theta=(\sqrt{V_i^\theta}+\varepsilon)^{-1}\). Since \(0<W_i^\theta\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\) and \(M_i^\theta\in L^2\), the covariance below is well defined. Expanding its definition gives

\[ F_i(\theta)=h_i(\theta)\,\mathbb{E}[W_i^\theta] +\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}(M_i^\theta,W_i^\theta). \tag{2.10}\]

Consequently, at an ordinary root,

\[ F_i(\theta^\star) =\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^\star}^{\mathrm{stat}}} (M_i^{\theta^\star},W_i^{\theta^\star}). \tag{2.11}\]

Thus an ordinary root is also an Adam root exactly when these componentwise covariances vanish. The identity isolates the calibration issue: centring the innovation centres the first memory, but does not by itself centre the normalised readout. It does not prove that any covariance is nonzero, locate an Adam equilibrium, or demonstrate root displacement for a particular Metropolis–Hastings model. Its role here is to motivate identifying the joint stationary memory law.

3 Markov augmentation and stationary memory law

3.1 The frozen augmented kernel

Fix \(\theta\). Let \(\mathsf Y=\mathbb{R}^d\times[0,\infty)^d\) be the memory space, with its Euclidean product norm, and let \(\mathsf Z=\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\) carry the product sigma-algebra. Define

\[ T_\theta(x',m,v)= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha m+(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x')\\ \beta v+(1-\beta)H_\theta(x')^{\odot2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{3.1}\]

For \(u=(m,v)\), also write \(T_\theta(x,u)=T_\theta(x,m,v)\). For the frozen recursions, \(Z_n=(X_n,M_n,V_n)\) has transition kernel

\[ Q_\theta((x,u),dx'\,du') =P_\theta(x,dx')\,\delta_{T_\theta(x',u)}(du'). \tag{3.2}\]

Conditional on the current state, the next observation has law \(P_\theta(x,\cdot)\) and the next memory is a deterministic measurable function of that observation and the current memory. Hence \(Z_n\) is Markov. For two memory states driven by the same observation,

\[ \begin{aligned} \|T_\theta(x',m,v)-T_\theta(x',\widetilde m,\widetilde v)\|^2 &=\alpha^2\|m-\widetilde m\|^2+\beta^2\|v-\widetilde v\|^2\\ &\leq\kappa^2\|(m,v)-(\widetilde m,\widetilde v)\|^2, \qquad \kappa=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}<1. \end{aligned} \tag{3.3}\]

This contracts the memory coordinates along a common driving path. It is not a contraction assertion for \(Q_\theta\) on the full augmented space: different initial driving states have not been coupled.

3.2 Identification of the stationary memories

Corollary 3.1 (Stationary memory representation) Under Assumption 2.1, \(Q_\theta\) has a unique invariant probability \(\nu_\theta\). Its driving marginal is \(\pi_\theta\) and

\[ \nu_\theta= \operatorname{Law}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}} (X_0^\theta,M^\theta,V^\theta), \tag{3.4}\]

where \(M^\theta,V^\theta\) are the stationary histories in Equation 2.7. Consequently,

\[ F(\theta)=\int_{\mathsf Z} \frac{m}{\sqrt v+\varepsilon}\, \nu_\theta(dx\,dm\,dv). \tag{3.5}\]

Proof. Define \(M_k^\theta\) and \(V_k^\theta\) by shifting the infinite series in Equation 2.7 to time \(k\). Their almost-sure convergence follows from Lemma 2.1 and stationarity. They satisfy the frozen recursions, while the joint process \((X_k^\theta,M_k^\theta,V_k^\theta)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\) is stationary. Its one-time law is therefore invariant for Equation 3.2.

For uniqueness, let \(\eta\) be another invariant probability of \(Q_\theta\). Its driving marginal must equal \(\pi_\theta\), by the uniqueness assumed in Assumption 2.1. Couple initial states with laws \(\eta\) and \(\nu_\theta\) so that their driving coordinates agree, and then use the same driving transitions for both memory recursions. Their memory difference after \(n\) steps is bounded by \(\kappa^n\|U_0-\widetilde U_0\|\). This tends to zero almost surely without a moment assumption on the two initial memories. For every bounded measurable test function that is uniformly Lipschitz in the memory coordinate, bounded convergence gives equality of the two stationary expectations. Such tests determine probability measures on \(\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\), hence \(\eta=\nu_\theta\). The integral representation follows from Lemma 2.1 and \(\varepsilon>0\).

This stationary identification does not require a geometric contraction estimate for the driving chain. Such an estimate is introduced in the next section to control distances between augmented transition laws from different initial driving states, and subsequently to compare kernels with different frozen parameters.

4 Wasserstein contraction of the augmented kernel

The preceding construction identifies a stationary law without requiring a metric contraction of the augmented process. We now impose assumptions that give a quantitative bound on its transition kernel. The argument applies to a contractive memory update more generally than the affine EMA recursion.

4.1 Metrics and a coupled driving chain

Let \((\mathsf X,d_X)\) be Polish and let \((\mathsf Y,d_Y)\) be a complete separable memory space. On \(\mathsf Z=\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\) use

\[ d_Z((x,u),(y,v))=d_X(x,y)+d_Y(u,v). \tag{4.1}\]

Write \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\) for probabilities with finite first moment in \(d_Z\). For \(\mu,\eta\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\), the \(1\)-Wasserstein distance satisfies the primal and Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual representations,

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{d_Z}(\mu,\eta) &=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal C(\mu,\eta)} \int d_Z(z,z')\,\gamma(dz\,dz')\\ &=\sup_{\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}\leq1} |\mu(f)-\eta(f)|. \end{aligned} \tag{4.2}\]

For a Markov kernel \(K\) write

\[ \tau_{d_Z}(K)=\sup_{z\ne z'} \frac{W_{d_Z}(K(z,\cdot),K(z',\cdot))}{d_Z(z,z')}. \tag{4.3}\]

Fix a parameter region \(\mathsf K\subseteq\Theta\). The constants below may depend on \(\mathsf K\), but not on \(\theta\in\mathsf K\).

Assumption 4.1 (Geometrically contracting driver coupling) For every \(\theta\in\mathsf K\) there is a Markovian coupling of two \(P_\theta\)-chains, with joint law denoted by \(\mathbb{P}^\theta_{x,y}\), such that for every \(n\geq0\) and \(x,y\in\mathsf X\),

\[ \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_X(X_n,\widetilde X_n) \leq C_P\rho_P^n d_X(x,y), \qquad C_P<\infty,\quad 0<\rho_P<1. \tag{4.4}\]

This is an estimate for one coupled pair of trajectories, at every time. A bound on \(W_{d_X}(P_\theta^n(x,\cdot),P_\theta^n(y,\cdot))\) for each \(n\), considered separately, does not by itself specify a consistent coupling of the intervening observations.

Assumption 4.2 (Contractive and spatially regular memory update) For \(\theta\in\mathsf K\), the update \(T_\theta:\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\to\mathsf Y\) is measurable and, with constants \(r<1\) and \(L_T<\infty\) independent of \(\theta\),

\[ d_Y(T_\theta(x,u),T_\theta(y,v)) \leq r\,d_Y(u,v)+L_Td_X(x,y). \tag{4.5}\]

For one, hence every, fixed \(z_0\in\mathsf Z\), assume \(Q_\theta(z_0,\cdot)\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\).

The last assumption is a one-step moment condition. It can be verified from a driver drift inequality and the growth of the memory forcing.

Theorem 4.1 (Wasserstein contraction lifts through contractive memory) Suppose Assumption 4.1 and Assumption 4.2 hold. For every \(\eta\in(\max\{\rho_P,r\},1)\) there is \(C_Z(\eta)<\infty\), uniform in \(\theta\in\mathsf K\), such that

\[ W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n(z,\cdot),Q_\theta^n(z',\cdot)) \leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n d_Z(z,z'), \qquad n\geq0. \tag{4.6}\]

In particular \(\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n)\leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n\). Each \(Q_\theta\) has a unique invariant distribution \(\nu_\theta\) in \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\), and

\[ W_{d_Z}(\delta_zQ_\theta^n,\nu_\theta) \leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n W_{d_Z}(\delta_z,\nu_\theta). \tag{4.7}\]

Proof. Fix \(\theta\). Couple two driving paths according to Assumption 4.1, and evolve the corresponding memories using those paths. Put \(e_n=\mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_Y(U_n,\widetilde U_n)\). By Equation 4.5 and induction,

\[ e_n\leq r^n d_Y(u,v) +L_T\sum_{j=1}^n r^{n-j} \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_X(X_j,\widetilde X_j). \]

Together with Equation 4.4 this gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_Z(Z_n,\widetilde Z_n) \leq {}& C_P\rho_P^n d_X(x,y)+r^n d_Y(u,v)\\ &+L_TC_P\sum_{j=1}^n r^{n-j}\rho_P^j d_X(x,y). \end{aligned} \tag{4.8}\]

For \(\eta>\max\{r,\rho_P\}\), the geometric convolution is bounded by \(c_\eta\eta^n\) with \(c_\eta<\infty\). The law of the coupled augmented paths is a coupling of the two \(n\)-step transition laws. Taking it as a candidate in the primal Wasserstein formula proves Equation 4.6.

At \(n=1\) this gives \(\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta)<\infty\). In conjunction with \(Q_\theta(z_0,\cdot)\in\mathcal P_1\), the triangle inequality shows that \(Q_\theta\) maps \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\) into itself. Choose \(m\) such that \(C_Z(\eta)\eta^m<1\). Then \(\mu\mapsto\mu Q_\theta^m\) is a strict contraction on the complete space \((\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z),W_{d_Z})\), so Banach’s fixed-point theorem supplies a unique fixed point \(\nu_\theta\). Since \(\nu_\theta Q_\theta\) is also fixed by \(Q_\theta^m\), uniqueness gives \(\nu_\theta Q_\theta=\nu_\theta\). Finally, apply Equation 4.6 to the coupling of \(\delta_z\) and \(\nu_\theta\).

When Assumption 2.1 holds with the corresponding finite moments, Corollary 3.1 identifies this invariant distribution with the law of the stationary infinite-history memories. Thus the Wasserstein construction gives a quantitative route to the same frozen expectation, without requiring a backward iteration in its proof. Uniqueness among all invariant probabilities in the affine EMA setting follows independently from the stationary construction and synchronous memory argument in Corollary 3.1; Theorem 4.1 itself asserts uniqueness in \(\mathcal P_1\).

4.2 Affine EMA updates and Lyapunov moments

For Equation 3.1, use \(d_Y(u,v)=\|u-v\|\) and write

\[ T_\theta(x,u)=Au+b_\theta(x),\quad A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha I_d,\beta I_d),\quad b_\theta(x)=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x)\\(1-\beta)H_\theta(x)^{\odot2}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.9}\]

If \(b_\theta\) is \(L_b\)-Lipschitz in \(d_X\), then Assumption 4.2 holds with \(r=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}\) and \(L_T=L_b\). In particular, a bound on the second moment of \(H_\theta\) is not alone a spatial Lipschitz estimate for \(b_\theta\) in an arbitrary metric. The metric and the innovation regularity must be checked together.

A drift inequality gives a complementary first-moment bound. Suppose for a measurable \(w:\mathsf X\to[1,\infty)\),

\[ P_\theta w\leq aw+B,\qquad \|T_\theta(x,u)\|\leq r\|u\|+c_0+c_1w(x), \qquad a,r<1. \tag{4.10}\]

Choose \(\delta\in(\max\{a,r\},1)\) and \(k>0\) sufficiently large that \((k+c_1)a\leq\delta k\). With

\[ \mathcal V(x,u)=1+kw(x)+\|u\|, \tag{4.11}\]

the augmented transition satisfies

\[ Q_\theta\mathcal V\leq\delta\mathcal V+L,\qquad L<\infty. \tag{4.12}\]

Indeed, \(Q_\theta\mathcal V(x,u)\leq1+(k+c_1)P_\theta w(x)+r\|u\|+c_0\), and substitution of Equation 4.10 gives the claim. If \(w(x)\) is comparable to \(1+d_X(x,x_0)\), this drift is naturally aligned with the first moment in \(d_Z\). For an invariant law with finite \(\mathcal V\)-moment, invariance yields \(\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)\leq L/(1-\delta)\). In the metric of Equation 4.1, the corresponding first eccentricity is

\[ \int d_Z(z_0,z)\,\nu_\theta(dz)<\infty, \]

as required for the \(p=1\) Wasserstein Poisson theory of [5].

4.3 The normalised Adam observable

There is a distinction between regularity of the memory update and regularity of its readout. The map \((m,v)\mapsto m/(\sqrt v+\varepsilon)\) is not globally Lipschitz in \(v\) under the ordinary Euclidean metric near \(v=0\). For a bounded innovation, however, a change of memory coordinates removes this difficulty without modifying the Adam recursion.

Assume, for the remainder of this subsection, that

\[ \|H_\theta(x)\|_\infty\leq B_H,\qquad \|H_\theta(x)-H_\theta(y)\|\leq L_Hd_X(x,y). \tag{4.13}\]

Put \(S_n=\sqrt{V_n}\) coordinatewise and use the forward-invariant memory set

\[ \mathsf Y_B=\{(m,s):\|m\|_\infty\leq B_H,\quad 0\leq s_i\leq B_H\}, \]

with metric \(d_Y((m,s),(m',s'))=\|m-m'\|+\|s-s'\|\). With initial memories \((M_0,S_0)\in\mathsf Y_B\), the update is

\[ \widehat T_\theta(x,m,s)= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha m+(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x)\\ \sqrt{\beta s^{\odot2}+(1-\beta)H_\theta(x)^{\odot2}} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.14}\]

For each coordinate, the second component is the Euclidean norm of \((\sqrt\beta\,s_i,\sqrt{1-\beta}\,H_{\theta,i}(x))\). The reverse triangle inequality therefore gives

\[ \begin{aligned} d_Y(\widehat T_\theta(x,u),\widehat T_\theta(y,v)) \leq {}&\max\{\alpha,\sqrt\beta\}\,d_Y(u,v)\\ &+\bigl(1-\alpha+\sqrt{1-\beta}\bigr)L_Hd_X(x,y). \end{aligned} \tag{4.15}\]

Thus the transformed memory still satisfies Assumption 4.2, with contraction coefficient \(\max\{\alpha,\sqrt\beta\}<1\). On \(\mathsf Y_B\) the Adam readout

\[ G(m,s)=\frac{m}{s+\varepsilon} \tag{4.16}\]

is Lipschitz:

\[ \|G(m,s)-G(m',s')\| \leq\varepsilon^{-1}\|m-m'\| +B_H\varepsilon^{-2}\|s-s'\|. \tag{4.17}\]

This is an alternative metric representation of the same finite-dimensional memory process, not a change in its update rule. It gives a direct Lipschitz observable for the Poisson equation when the innovation is bounded, as for an acceptance-rate residual. For unbounded innovations the affine \((m,v)\) representation still admits Theorem 4.1 under its stated assumptions; the Lipschitz property of the Adam readout must then be addressed separately.

5 Parameter perturbation of the augmented process

The contraction theorem is stated for each frozen parameter, with constants uniform on a specified region. We now compare two such frozen kernels, \(Q_\theta\) and \(Q_\vartheta\). The comparison is an operator calculation on Lipschitz test functions; it does not require constructing an additional coupling of the augmented transitions.

5.1 One-step kernel regularity

For \(f:\mathsf Z\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(z=(x,u)\), write

\[ f_{\theta,u}(y)=f(y,T_\theta(y,u)). \tag{5.1}\]

By Equation 3.2,

\[ Q_\theta f(x,u)=P_\theta f_{\theta,u}(x). \]

Adding and subtracting \(P_\vartheta f_{\theta,u}(x)\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f(x,u) ={}&(P_\theta-P_\vartheta)f_{\theta,u}(x)\\ &+P_\vartheta(f_{\theta,u}-f_{\vartheta,u})(x). \end{aligned} \tag{5.2}\]

Under Assumption 4.2, \(\|f_{\theta,u}\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_X)}\leq(1+L_T)\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}\), and

\[ |f_{\theta,u}(y)-f_{\vartheta,u}(y)| \leq\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)} d_Y(T_\theta(y,u),T_\vartheta(y,u)). \]

Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality therefore yields

\[ \begin{aligned} &W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta((x,u),\cdot),Q_\vartheta((x,u),\cdot))\\ &\quad\leq (1+L_T)W_{d_X}(P_\theta(x,\cdot),P_\vartheta(x,\cdot))\\ &\qquad+P_\vartheta\!\left[ d_Y(T_\theta(\cdot,u),T_\vartheta(\cdot,u)) \right](x). \end{aligned} \tag{5.3}\]

The first term measures the change in the driving kernel; the second measures the change in the memory update under the same driving law. For the affine EMA, the second term depends only on \(b_\theta-b_\vartheta\), since \(A\) is fixed. In the square-root coordinates of Equation 4.14 it can be controlled instead by the parameter difference of the original innovation \(H_\theta\).

Assumption 5.1 (Driver-level parameter regularity) On a parameter region \(\mathsf K\), assume there is a measurable \(w:\mathsf X\to[1,\infty)\) and constants \(L_P,L_{T,\theta}<\infty\), uniform over \(\theta,\vartheta\in\mathsf K\), such that

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{d_X}(P_\theta(x,\cdot),P_\vartheta(x,\cdot)) &\leq L_P\|\theta-\vartheta\|w(x),\\ P_\vartheta[d_Y(T_\theta(\cdot,u),T_\vartheta(\cdot,u))](x) &\leq L_{T,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|w(x) \end{aligned} \tag{5.4}\]

for all \(x\) and \(u\) in the memory space under consideration. Let \(\mathcal V:\mathsf Z\to[1,\infty)\) dominate \(w(x)\) and suppose the family \((Q_\theta)_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\) has uniform geometric Wasserstein contraction as in Theorem 4.1.

The bounds in Assumption 5.1 are verification assumptions on the driver and forcing, not consequences of frozen contraction.

Proposition 5.1 (One-step regularity of augmented kernels) Under Assumption 4.2 and Assumption 5.1,

\[ \gamma_{\theta,\vartheta} :=\sup_{z\in\mathsf Z} \frac{W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta(z,\cdot),Q_\vartheta(z,\cdot))} {\mathcal V(z)} \leq L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|, \tag{5.5}\]

with \(L_Q=(1+L_T)L_P+L_{T,\theta}\). Equivalently, for every \(d_Z\)-Lipschitz test \(f\),

\[ |(Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f(z)| \leq L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|\mathcal V(z) \|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}. \tag{5.6}\]

Proof. Apply Equation 5.3, substitute Equation 5.4 and divide by \(\mathcal V(z)\geq w(x)\). The equivalent operator estimate is the dual representation of \(W_{d_Z}\).

5.2 Perturbation of invariant laws

The geometric estimate of Theorem 4.1 and the weighted one-step bound are the hypotheses appearing in the Wasserstein perturbation framework of [4, Theorem 3.1]. The stationary specialisation can also be seen directly from the operator decomposition.

Corollary 5.1 (Lipschitz dependence of augmented invariant laws) Assume Proposition 5.1 and suppose the invariant laws satisfy

\[ \sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)\leq M_{\mathcal V}<\infty. \tag{5.7}\]

Then for every \(\theta,\vartheta\in\mathsf K\),

\[ W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta) \leq \frac{C_Z(\eta)L_QM_{\mathcal V}}{1-\eta} \|\theta-\vartheta\|. \tag{5.8}\]

Proof. Fix a \(1\)-Lipschitz \(f\). Since \(\nu_\theta\) and \(\nu_\vartheta\) are invariant,

\[ \begin{aligned} |\nu_\theta(f)-\nu_\vartheta(f)| \leq{}& |(\nu_\theta-\nu_\vartheta)(Q_\theta^n f)|\\ &+|\nu_\vartheta(Q_\theta^nf-Q_\vartheta^nf)|. \end{aligned} \]

The first term is at most \(C_Z(\eta)\eta^n W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta)\). For the second use

\[ Q_\theta^n-Q_\vartheta^n =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}Q_\vartheta^j (Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)Q_\theta^{n-1-j}. \tag{5.9}\]

The stationarity of \(\nu_\vartheta\), Equation 5.6 and the bound \(\|Q_\theta^k f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^k\) give

\[ |\nu_\vartheta(Q_\theta^nf-Q_\vartheta^nf)| \leq C_Z(\eta)L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|\, \nu_\vartheta(\mathcal V)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\eta^{n-1-j}. \]

Let \(n\to\infty\). The first term tends to zero, and the geometric sum is bounded by \((1-\eta)^{-1}\). Taking the supremum over \(f\) proves Equation 5.8.

Uniform drift constants in Equation 4.12, together with suitable moment conditions, provide one route to Equation 5.7. The constant in Equation 5.8 is not asserted to be uniform on parameter regions where the contraction, moment or perturbation constants diverge.

5.3 Regularity of the stationary Adam field

Suppose \(G_\theta:\mathsf Z\to\mathbb{R}^d\) has a uniform Lipschitz seminorm and satisfies

\[ \|G_\theta(z)-G_\vartheta(z)\| \leq L_{G,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|\mathcal V(z). \tag{5.10}\]

For \(F(\theta)=\nu_\theta(G_\theta)\), add and subtract \(\nu_\vartheta(G_\theta)\) to obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} \|F(\theta)-F(\vartheta)\| \leq{}& \|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\, W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta)\\ &+\nu_\vartheta(\|G_\theta-G_\vartheta\|). \end{aligned} \tag{5.11}\]

Thus Corollary 5.1 gives local Lipschitz regularity of \(F\) under Equation 5.10. For the square-root memory coordinates of Equation 4.16 the readout is independent of \(\theta\), so the second term vanishes. This is a regularity conclusion for the stationary field, not yet an ODE approximation for the changing-parameter recursion.

6 Poisson equations and changing parameters

The frozen augmented process has a stationary law \(\nu_\theta\) and, under Theorem 4.1, geometric Wasserstein contraction. For the controlled recursion, the remaining question is whether the centred observation can be accumulated along a path on which \(\theta\) changes. We work with Lipschitz functions throughout; no weighted total-variation convergence of the augmented process is assumed.

6.1 Frozen Poisson equation on the Lipschitz space

For a scalar observable \(G_\theta\in\operatorname{Lip}(\mathsf Z,d_Z)\), put

\[ F(\theta)=\nu_\theta(G_\theta),\qquad \bar G_\theta=G_\theta-F(\theta). \tag{6.1}\]

Vector-valued observables are handled coordinatewise. Consider the frozen Poisson equation

\[ u_\theta-Q_\theta u_\theta=\bar G_\theta. \tag{6.2}\]

Under geometric Wasserstein contraction, finite first eccentricity and the corresponding first-moment conditions, [5, Theorem 3.4] applies to this centred Lipschitz observable. In particular, a solution is given by

\[ u_\theta(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty Q_\theta^n\bar G_\theta(z), \tag{6.3}\]

with \(\nu_\theta(u_\theta)=0\) and

\[ \|u_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)} \leq\Lambda_\theta\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}, \qquad \Lambda_\theta:=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n) \leq\frac{C_Z(\eta)}{1-\eta}. \tag{6.4}\]

The series and its normalisation are understood in the integrable Lipschitz class supplied by the theorem. The regularity assumption concerns the forcing observable \(G_\theta\), not only the memory update \(T_\theta\). The square-root coordinates and bounded-innovation case of Equation 4.14–Equation 4.17 give one direct verification for Adam’s normalised readout.

6.2 Parameter differences of Poisson solutions

The one-step estimate Equation 5.6 is sufficient for the invariant-law perturbation argument. It is not, by itself, an estimate of the Lipschitz seminorm of \((Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f\) as a function of the state. We therefore record explicitly the additional operator condition used in the following restricted-space result.

Assumption 6.1 (Lipschitz-space operator regularity) On \(\mathsf K\) suppose the quantities \(\Lambda_\theta\) in Equation 6.4 are uniformly bounded by \(\Lambda<\infty\), and that

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}&\leq L_G,\\ \|G_\theta-G_\vartheta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}} &\leq L_{G,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|,\\ \|(Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}} &\leq L_{\mathrm{op}}\|\theta-\vartheta\| \|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}} \end{aligned} \tag{6.5}\]

for every Lipschitz \(f\). The seminorm of a constant is zero, so the last inequality is unaffected by adding constants to \(f\). Assume the corresponding Poisson solutions are well defined, as above.

This is an explicit sufficient hypothesis, not a consequence claimed from Equation 5.6. It can be replaced by a weaker weighted resolvent estimate if such an estimate is verified directly. The approach parallels the parameter-dependent Poisson argument of [6], formulated there in a weighted sup-norm rather than the present Lipschitz seminorm.

Proposition 6.1 (Parameter regularity in the restricted Lipschitz space) Under Assumption 6.1, fix \(z_0\in\mathsf Z\) and choose the normalisation \(u_\theta(z_0)=0\) for every \(\theta\in\mathsf K\). Then

\[ \|u_\theta-u_\vartheta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}} \leq\Lambda\left(L_{G,\theta} +L_{\mathrm{op}}\Lambda L_G\right)\|\theta-\vartheta\|. \tag{6.6}\]

In particular,

\[ |u_\theta(z)-u_\vartheta(z)| \leq\Lambda\left(L_{G,\theta} +L_{\mathrm{op}}\Lambda L_G\right) \|\theta-\vartheta\|d_Z(z,z_0). \tag{6.7}\]

Proof. Subtract the two Poisson equations, writing \(v=u_\theta-u_\vartheta\):

\[ (I-Q_\theta)v= (G_\theta-G_\vartheta) -\bigl(F(\theta)-F(\vartheta)\bigr) +(Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)u_\vartheta. \tag{6.8}\]

The right-hand side has zero \(\nu_\theta\)-mean: this follows by integrating the identity against \(\nu_\theta\) and using invariance. Its Lipschitz seminorm is at most

\[ \left(L_{G,\theta} +L_{\mathrm{op}}\|u_\vartheta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\right) \|\theta-\vartheta\|. \]

The frozen Poisson estimate Equation 6.4, applied to this centred right-hand side, bounds the Lipschitz seminorm of its solution by \(\Lambda\) times the displayed quantity. Use \(\|u_\vartheta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq\Lambda L_G\) to obtain Equation 6.6. Solutions of the centred Poisson equation differ by constants; the common value at \(z_0\) identifies \(v\) with the normalised solution. The pointwise estimate follows by the definition of the Lipschitz seminorm.

The distinction between Equation 5.6 and Equation 6.5 matters for the intended application. The first has already been reduced to driver-level parameter estimates. The second still requires verification, or an alternative weighted resolvent argument, before the stated Poisson parameter conclusion can be invoked.

6.3 The feedback term in the controlled recursion

Return to the changing-parameter augmented process,

\[ Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n\sim Q_{\theta_n}(Z_n,\cdot), \qquad \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1}G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1}). \tag{6.9}\]

For Adam, \(G_\theta\) is the normalised readout on the augmented memory. The order of the transition and parameter update in Equation 6.9 is the one used in Equation 2.6. Define

\[ \Delta M_{n+1} =u_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-Q_{\theta_n}u_{\theta_n}(Z_n). \tag{6.10}\]

Then \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=0\). Put \(\phi_\theta=Q_\theta u_\theta\). Evaluating Equation 6.2 at \(Z_{n+1}\) and adding and subtracting \(\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)\) gives

\[ G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-F(\theta_n) =\Delta M_{n+1} +\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)-\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1}). \tag{6.11}\]

Under weighted summation, the last two terms telescope except for boundary, gain-variation and parameter-change terms. The latter involve

\[ \phi_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})- \phi_{\theta_{n+1}}(Z_{n+1}). \tag{6.12}\]

The operator decomposition

\[ \phi_\theta-\phi_\vartheta =(Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)u_\theta+ Q_\vartheta(u_\theta-u_\vartheta) \tag{6.13}\]

shows how Equation 5.6 and Proposition 6.1 enter together. If these differences have the required weighted growth bound, and \(\|\theta_{n+1}-\theta_n\|=O(\gamma_{n+1})\), the parameter-change remainder is of second order after multiplication by the SA gain. Controlling the sum still requires integrability along the adaptive path.

The standard Poisson–martingale method of controlled stochastic approximation [6–8] then suggests the limiting equation

\[ \dot\theta=F(\theta). \tag{6.14}\]

No asymptotic pseudo-trajectory or parameter-convergence theorem is claimed here. Such a theorem additionally requires a stability or localisation argument for \((\theta_n)\), a gain condition, martingale moment estimates and control of the weighted remainders in Equation 6.11. The preceding results identify the precise frozen and parameter-dependent estimates that would enter that proof; they do not replace it.

7 Discussion

Adam’s stationary calibration field averages a nonlinear readout of joint first- and second-moment memories. Linear momentum preserves \(h\), whereas Equation 2.10 shows why centring the ordinary innovation need not centre Adam’s readout. The field depends on the joint stationary memory law and hence potentially on temporal dependence, beyond the invariant one-time innovation marginal. No model-specific Metropolis–Hastings root displacement has been established here.

For a frozen Markov driver with a unique invariant probability and a finite second innovation moment, the fixed-gain memories admit a finite-dimensional Markov augmentation with a unique invariant law. The integral in Equation 3.5 represents the stationary Adam field under this law. Under the additional driver coupling and spatial regularity assumptions of Theorem 4.1, the augmented transition laws also converge at a geometric rate in Wasserstein distance. This forward argument does not require time reversal, and it supplies the quantitative contraction used in the invariant-measure perturbation and frozen Poisson results of [4, 5].

The Markovian iterated-function-system results of [9] provide a useful comparison. Their analysis allows contraction conditions formulated through random Lipschitz coefficients and stationary averages, rather than a uniform metric contraction at every memory update. It therefore motivates investigating which of the geometric coupling and regularity assumptions in the present framework can be weakened while retaining the quantitative bounds required for Wasserstein perturbation and Poisson control. We do not claim that the rates or conclusions of [9] imply the Wasserstein Dobrushin estimates used here.

Parameter regularity can likewise be reduced to properties of the driver and its forcing. In particular, Equation 5.2 separates the change of the driving transition from the change of the memory update, and Corollary 5.1 transfers this estimate to the augmented invariant law. These calculations do not impose weighted total-variation ergodicity on the augmented process. Contractive deterministic memories can converge in Wasserstein while their transition laws remain mutually singular at every finite time.

For bounded innovations, the square-root second-moment coordinate gives a natural metric in which both the memory update and Adam’s normalised readout are Lipschitz. This is relevant to acceptance-rate calibration, where the innovation is bounded. More general unbounded innovations may require different metrics or additional regularity of the readout.

The parameter-dependent Poisson statement in Proposition 6.1 uses an additional Lipschitz-operator perturbation bound. The weaker weighted pointwise kernel estimate, although sufficient for the invariant-law result, does not establish this bound automatically. Closing that distinction and then controlling the adaptive martingale and summation-by-parts remainders are the remaining steps towards ODE tracking of \(F\). Convergence to a root of \(h\), or even to a single equilibrium of \(F\), remains a separate question. The present results establish the stationary covariance identity and a quantitative framework for studying its behaviour under controlled Markov noise, without identifying a particular adaptive-MCMC equilibrium.
