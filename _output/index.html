Wasserstein stability and ODE tracking for Markov-driven stochastic approximation with contractive memory

Author
Affiliation

Jordan Simbananiye

University College London (UCL) - CCMI CDT

Published

19 November 2026

Abstract

We study Markov-driven stochastic approximation with persistent, contractive memory. A trajectory coupling of the driving chain lifts to geometric Wasserstein contraction of the augmented kernel, yielding an invariant law and quantitative convergence. Weighted kernel perturbations give Lipschitz dependence of the invariant law and log-Lipschitz parameter regularity of centred Poisson solutions. Under quadratic drift, parameter stability, bounded increments and logarithmically corrected gain summability, a Poisson–martingale decomposition yields tracking of the stationary mean-field ODE. Adam adaptation in MCMC provides a motivating application: stationary EMA memories exist under weaker frozen moment assumptions, and the Adam field contains a covariance correction at Robbins–Monro roots.

1 Introduction

Adaptive MCMC commonly calibrates a sampling-kernel parameter by Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation [1, 2]. For example, a proposal scale can be adjusted using an acceptance-rate residual relative to a prescribed target. With the parameter frozen at \(\theta\), the stationary expectation of that residual defines a field \(h(\theta)\); the intended calibration equation is \(h(\theta)=0\). More generally, adaptation seeks a root of a prescribed stationary mean-field equation.

Adam replaces the ordinary innovation by a nonlinear function of exponentially weighted first- and second-moment estimates. The memory coefficients are fixed, while the parameter gain decreases. Linear momentum preserves the frozen mean field in expectation, but Adam’s random normaliser is coupled to its numerator. Its stationary field \(F(\theta)\) need not coincide with \(h(\theta)\), and the two calibration equations need not have the same roots.

Identifying this stationary field, proving that the actual stochastic recursion tracks an ODE, and proving convergence to a particular equilibrium are distinct questions. An ODE approximation with field \(F\) does not establish convergence to a root of \(h\), or even convergence to a single equilibrium of \(F\). Dereich–Jentzen–Kassing [3, Definition 1.3 and Theorem 1.5] identify the Adam field and establish an ODE approximation under IID innovations, with moment, regularity and gain assumptions and \(\alpha^2<\beta<1\). Their analysis provides the IID benchmark; it does not supply the corresponding result for parameter-controlled Markov observations.

In adaptive MCMC the innovations are dependent and their transition law changes with the adaptation parameter. Freezing that parameter gives a homogeneous Markov driver. We study the broader family of Markov-driven stochastic approximation algorithms with persistent, contractive memory. Augmenting the driver by the memory coordinates gives a Markov kernel to which a forward coupling argument transfers geometric Wasserstein contraction.

Geometric Wasserstein contraction and weighted one-step parameter regularity control the invariant laws [4]. Hofstadler’s Poisson construction [5] supplies the frozen resolvent, while eccentricity gives weighted-growth bounds. We combine these estimates to obtain a log-Lipschitz parameter modulus for the augmented Poisson solution. A Poisson–martingale decomposition then yields ODE tracking under quadratic drift, bounded increments, diminishing gains and parameter stability. The martingale identity follows the calculation in [6, Lemma 4].

The general recursion and notation are introduced in Chapter 2, followed by the lifting theorem in Chapter 3. Stationary Adam calibration is developed in Chapter 4 under its own frozen moment assumptions. The parameter perturbation and Poisson results in Chapter 5 then lead to the ODE-tracking theorem in Chapter 6. The covariance identity remains the calibration question motivating these constructions.

2 General framework and preliminaries

2.1 Markov-driven recursions with memory

Let \((\mathsf X,\mathcal A)\) and \((\mathsf Y,\mathcal B)\) be standard Borel spaces for the driver and memory, and let \(\Theta\subseteq\mathbb R^d\) be a Borel parameter set. For each \(\theta\in\Theta\), let \(P_\theta\) be a Markov kernel on \(\mathsf X\), \(T_\theta:\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\to\mathsf Y\) a memory update and \(G_\theta:\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\to\mathbb R^d\) a readout. Assume these objects are jointly measurable in parameter and state. With \(X_n,U_n,\theta_n\) adapted to \((\mathcal F_n)\), consider

\[ \begin{aligned} X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n&\sim P_{\theta_n}(X_n,\cdot),\\ U_{n+1}&=T_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1},U_n),\\ \theta_{n+1}&=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1}G_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1},U_{n+1}), \end{aligned} \tag{2.1}\]

where \((\gamma_n)\) are positive deterministic gains; write \(G_\theta(z)=G_\theta(x,u)\) for \(z=(x,u)\). The memory update persists on the observation timescale, while the parameter gain will decrease. Set \(\mathsf Z=\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\) and \(Z_n=(X_n,U_n)\). For a frozen parameter, the augmented transition kernel is

\[ Q_\theta((x,u),dx'\,du') =P_\theta(x,dx')\,\delta_{T_\theta(x',u)}(du'). \tag{2.2}\]

Marginalising over the memory coordinate gives \(Q_\theta((x,u),A\times\mathsf Y)=P_\theta(x,A)\) for every \(A\in\mathcal A\). If \(\eta\) is invariant for \(Q_\theta\) and \(\eta_X\) is its driver marginal, invariance gives \(\eta_X(A)=\int P_\theta(x,A)\,\eta_X(dx)\). Thus \(\eta_X\) is invariant for \(P_\theta\) and equals \(\pi_\theta\) whenever the driver has a unique invariant probability \(\pi_\theta\). Consequently, for a driver-only readout \(G_\theta(x,u)=H_\theta(x)\) with \(\pi_\theta\)-integrable \(H_\theta\), one has \(\eta(G_\theta)=\pi_\theta(H_\theta)=h(\theta)\), preserving the ordinary Robbins–Monro mean field.

The next memory is a deterministic function of the new observation and the current memory, so the frozen augmented process is Markov. The controlled recursion has conditional transition \(Q_{\theta_n}\).

Write \(\mu(f)=\int f\,d\mu\) when this integral exists. The action of a kernel on a probability measure is

\[ (\mu P_\theta)(A)=\int_{\mathsf X}P_\theta(x,A)\,\mu(dx),\qquad A\in\mathcal A. \tag{2.3}\]

An invariant probability \(\pi_\theta\) satisfies

\[ \pi_\theta P_\theta=\pi_\theta. \tag{2.4}\]

Thus one transition preserves the marginal \(\pi_\theta\) when the parameter is fixed. In the usual MCMC setting the sampling kernels share a target \(\pi_\theta=\pi\), although their transition dependence changes with \(\theta\).

The same notation applies to \(Q_\theta\) and its invariant probabilities. Whenever \(Q_\theta\) has an invariant law \(\nu_\theta\) and \(G_\theta\) is \(\nu_\theta\)-integrable, define the stationary mean field

\[ F(\theta)=\nu_\theta(G_\theta). \tag{2.5}\]

It is this field that enters the limiting ODE for the recursion with memory. Existence and regularity of \(\nu_\theta\) and \(F\) will follow under the assumptions stated below.

To describe memory contraction, equip \(\mathsf Y\) with a metric \(d_Y\) generating \(\mathcal B\), and fix a parameter region \(\mathsf K\subseteq\Theta\).

Definition 2.1 (Uniformly contractive memory) A memory update \(T_\theta:\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\to\mathsf Y\) is uniformly contractive in its memory argument on \(\mathsf K\) if, for some \(0\leq r<1\),

\[ d_Y(T_\theta(x,u),T_\theta(x,v))\leq r\,d_Y(u,v) \tag{2.6}\]

for every \(x\in\mathsf X\), \(u,v\in\mathsf Y\) and \(\theta\in\mathsf K\). Thus \(u\mapsto T_\theta(x,u)\) is \(r\)-Lipschitz uniformly over the driving observation and parameter.

This gives geometric forgetting of initial memory states along a common driving trajectory. Indeed, if \(U_{n+1}=T_\theta(X_{n+1},U_n)\) and \(\widetilde U_{n+1}=T_\theta(X_{n+1},\widetilde U_n)\), induction gives

\[ d_Y(U_n,\widetilde U_n)\leq r^n d_Y(U_0,\widetilde U_0). \tag{2.7}\]

To compare memories along different driving trajectories, the lifting theorem will also require spatial regularity of \(T_\theta\) in its observation argument.

2.2 Wasserstein distance and Lipschitz functions

For the Wasserstein results, equip \(\mathsf X\) and \(\mathsf Y\) with complete separable metrics \(d_X,d_Y\) generating their sigma-algebras. On \(\mathsf Z=\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\) use

\[ d_Z((x,u),(y,v))=d_X(x,y)+d_Y(u,v). \tag{2.8}\]

Write \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\) for probabilities with finite first moment in \(d_Z\). For \(\mu,\eta\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\), let \(\mathcal C(\mu,\eta)\) denote their couplings. The \(1\)-Wasserstein distance satisfies the primal and Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual representations,

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{d_Z}(\mu,\eta) &=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal C(\mu,\eta)} \int d_Z(z,z')\,\gamma(dz\,dz')\\ &=\sup_{\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}\leq1} |\mu(f)-\eta(f)|. \end{aligned} \tag{2.9}\]

For a real-valued function \(f\), its Lipschitz seminorm is

\[ \|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)} =\sup_{z\ne z'}\frac{|f(z)-f(z')|}{d_Z(z,z')}. \tag{2.10}\]

For a Markov kernel \(K\) write

\[ \tau_{d_Z}(K)=\sup_{z\ne z'} \frac{W_{d_Z}(K(z,\cdot),K(z',\cdot))}{d_Z(z,z')}. \tag{2.11}\]

By duality, \(\|Kf\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq\tau_{d_Z}(K)\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\). These are the distance and contraction coefficient used in the perturbation theory of [4]. Constants in the subsequent estimates are uniform on a specified parameter region \(\mathsf K\subseteq\Theta\).

2.3 Weighted norms and centred Poisson equations

For a measurable weight \(\mathcal V:\mathsf Z\to[1,\infty)\), write

\[ \|f\|_{\mathcal V}=\sup_z\frac{|f(z)|}{\mathcal V(z)}. \tag{2.12}\]

A Lyapunov inequality \(Q_\theta\mathcal V\leq a\mathcal V+b\), with \(0\leq a<1\) and \(b<\infty\), controls growth under iteration. Weighted one-step kernel differences will be measured against \(\mathcal V\) on Lipschitz test functions. This keeps the Wasserstein estimates and the weighted-norm parameter analysis of [7] in a common setting.

For \(\bar G_\theta=G_\theta-F(\theta)\), a centred Poisson solution satisfies

\[ u_\theta-Q_\theta u_\theta=\bar G_\theta, \qquad \nu_\theta(u_\theta)=0. \tag{2.13}\]

The Neumann-series representation is

\[ u_\theta(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \bigl[Q_\theta^nG_\theta(z)-F(\theta)\bigr], \tag{2.14}\]

when the centred iterates are summable. Geometric Wasserstein contraction will give this summability for Lipschitz observables, as in [5, Theorem 3.4]. The bounds needed for parameter comparison and adaptive tracking are developed after the contraction theorem.

3 Wasserstein contraction of the augmented kernel

A contracting Markov driver and a contractive memory update give geometric contraction of the full augmented process. The proof couples the driver trajectories and propagates their separation through the memory recursion.

3.1 Coupling assumptions and the lifting theorem

Fix a parameter region \(\mathsf K\subseteq\Theta\). The constants below may depend on \(\mathsf K\), but not on \(\theta\in\mathsf K\).

Assumption 3.1 (Geometrically contracting driver coupling) For every \(\theta\in\mathsf K\) there is a Markovian coupling of two \(P_\theta\)-chains, with joint law denoted by \(\mathbb{P}^\theta_{x,y}\), such that for every \(n\geq0\) and \(x,y\in\mathsf X\),

\[ \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_X(X_n,\widetilde X_n) \leq C_P\rho_P^n d_X(x,y), \qquad C_P<\infty,\quad 0<\rho_P<1. \tag{3.1}\]

This is an estimate for one coupled pair of trajectories, at every time. A bound on \(W_{d_X}(P_\theta^n(x,\cdot),P_\theta^n(y,\cdot))\) for each \(n\), considered separately, does not by itself specify a consistent coupling of the intervening observations.

Assumption 3.2 (Spatial regularity and first moments for contractive memory) The memory update is uniformly contractive on \(\mathsf K\) in the sense of Definition 2.1, with coefficient \(r\). In addition, there is a constant \(L_T<\infty\), independent of \(\theta\in\mathsf K\), such that

\[ d_Y(T_\theta(x,u),T_\theta(y,u))\leq L_Td_X(x,y) \tag{3.2}\]

for all \(x,y\in\mathsf X\) and \(u\in\mathsf Y\).

For one, hence every, fixed \(z_0\in\mathsf Z\), assume \(Q_\theta(z_0,\cdot)\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\).

By the triangle inequality, memory contraction and spatial regularity together give

\[ d_Y(T_\theta(x,u),T_\theta(y,v)) \leq r\,d_Y(u,v)+L_Td_X(x,y). \tag{3.3}\]

The one-step moment condition can be verified from a driver drift inequality and the growth of the memory forcing, as shown in Section 3.2.

Theorem 3.1 (Wasserstein contraction lifts through contractive memory) Suppose Assumption 3.1 and Assumption 3.2 hold. For every \(\eta\in(\max\{\rho_P,r\},1)\) there is \(C_Z(\eta)<\infty\), uniform in \(\theta\in\mathsf K\), such that

\[ W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n(z,\cdot),Q_\theta^n(z',\cdot)) \leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n d_Z(z,z'), \qquad n\geq0. \tag{3.4}\]

In particular \(\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n)\leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n\). Each \(Q_\theta\) has a unique invariant distribution \(\nu_\theta\) in \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\), and

\[ W_{d_Z}(\delta_zQ_\theta^n,\nu_\theta) \leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^n W_{d_Z}(\delta_z,\nu_\theta). \tag{3.5}\]

Proof. Fix \(\theta\). Couple two driving paths according to Assumption 3.1, and evolve the corresponding memories using those paths. Put \(e_n=\mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_Y(U_n,\widetilde U_n)\). By Equation 3.3 and induction,

\[ e_n\leq r^n d_Y(u,v) +L_T\sum_{j=1}^n r^{n-j} \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_X(X_j,\widetilde X_j). \]

Together with Equation 3.1 this gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}^\theta_{x,y}d_Z(Z_n,\widetilde Z_n) \leq {}& C_P\rho_P^n d_X(x,y)+r^n d_Y(u,v)\\ &+L_TC_P\sum_{j=1}^n r^{n-j}\rho_P^j d_X(x,y). \end{aligned} \tag{3.6}\]

For \(\eta>\max\{r,\rho_P\}\), the geometric convolution satisfies

\[ \sum_{j=1}^n r^{n-j}\rho_P^j =\eta^n\sum_{j=1}^n(r/\eta)^{n-j}(\rho_P/\eta)^j \leq\frac{\rho_P}{\eta-\rho_P}\eta^n. \]

Thus all three terms are bounded geometrically in \(n\). The law of the coupled augmented paths is a coupling of the two \(n\)-step transition laws. Taking it as a candidate in the primal Wasserstein formula proves Equation 3.4.

At \(n=1\) this gives \(\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta)<\infty\). In conjunction with \(Q_\theta(z_0,\cdot)\in\mathcal P_1\), the triangle inequality shows that \(Q_\theta\) maps \(\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\) into itself. Choose \(m\) such that \(C_Z(\eta)\eta^m<1\). Then \(\mu\mapsto\mu Q_\theta^m\) is a strict contraction on the complete space \((\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z),W_{d_Z})\), so Banach’s fixed-point theorem supplies a unique fixed point \(\nu_\theta\). Since \(\nu_\theta Q_\theta\) is also fixed by \(Q_\theta^m\), uniqueness gives \(\nu_\theta Q_\theta=\nu_\theta\). Finally, apply Equation 3.4 to the coupling of \(\delta_z\) and \(\nu_\theta\).

3.2 First-moment verification

A driver drift inequality and the growth of the memory forcing provide a convenient way to verify augmented moment bounds.

Lemma 3.2 (Drift lifted through memory) Suppose the memory space is a subset of a normed space. For finite nonnegative constants \(B,c_0,c_1\) and a measurable \(w:\mathsf X\to[1,\infty)\), assume

\[ P_\theta w\leq aw+B,\qquad \|T_\theta(x,u)\|\leq r\|u\|+c_0+c_1w(x), \qquad 0\leq a,r<1. \tag{3.7}\]

Choose \(\delta\in(\max\{a,r\},1)\) and \(k>0\) sufficiently large that \((k+c_1)a\leq\delta k\). With

\[ \mathcal V(x,u)=1+kw(x)+\|u\|, \tag{3.8}\]

the augmented transition satisfies

\[ Q_\theta\mathcal V\leq\delta\mathcal V+L,\qquad L<\infty. \tag{3.9}\]

The calculation is given in Appendix B.

With the norm metric on memory, if \(w(x)\) is comparable to \(1+d_X(x,x_0)\), this drift is naturally aligned with the first moment in \(d_Z\). For an invariant law with finite \(\mathcal V\)-moment, invariance yields \(\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)\leq L/(1-\delta)\). In the metric of Equation 2.8, the corresponding first eccentricity is

\[ \int d_Z(z_0,z)\,\nu_\theta(dz)<\infty, \]

as required for the \(p=1\) Wasserstein Poisson theory of [5].

4 Stationary calibration and Adam

4.1 Frozen kernels and probability laws

For the driver family of Chapter 2, let \(H:\Theta\times\mathsf X\to\mathbb R^d\) be a measurable innovation and write \(H_\theta(x)=H(\theta,x)\). The stationary EMA construction uses only the following frozen assumptions on the standard Borel driver space.

Assumption 4.1 (Frozen driver and innovation moment) For each \(\theta\in\Theta\), \(P_\theta\) has a unique invariant probability \(\pi_\theta\), and

\[ \pi_\theta(\|H_\theta\|^2)<\infty. \]

All subsequent stationary constructions are pointwise in \(\theta\); no regularity of the family of invariant laws is assumed here. Under Assumption 4.1, the consistent stationary finite-dimensional laws specified by \(\pi_\theta\) and \(P_\theta\) extend, by the Kolmogorov extension theorem, to a two-sided stationary Markov chain \((X_k^\theta)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\) on this standard Borel space. Denote its law on \((\mathsf{X}^{\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal A^{\otimes\mathbb{Z}})\) by \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\). Its one-time marginal is \(\pi_\theta\), and its forward transition conditional on the entire past is \(P_\theta\). The marginal \(\pi_\theta\) describes one observation; \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\) also describes temporal dependence and is generally not a product law.

The ordinary frozen mean field is

\[ h(\theta)=\pi_\theta(H_\theta) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}[H_\theta(X_0^\theta)]. \tag{4.1}\]

A Robbins–Monro root \(\theta^\star\) satisfies \(h(\theta^\star)=0\). For acceptance-rate calibration one may use \(H_\theta(x)=a_\theta(x)-a^\star\), where \(a_\theta(x)\) is the conditional expected acceptance probability and \(a^\star\) is the target rate. If the observed residual instead uses proposals or acceptance indicators, the driving state must include the variables needed to make that observation a function of its current state; the invariant law in Equation 4.1 then refers to that enlarged driver.

4.2 Fixed-gain memories and the stationary field

For context, a controlled recursion generates observations according to

\[ X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n\sim P_{\theta_n}(X_n,\cdot), \tag{4.2}\]

with \(\theta_n,X_n\) measurable with respect to \(\mathcal F_n\). Ordinary Robbins–Monro adaptation takes the form

\[ \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1}H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1}). \tag{4.3}\]

Here \((\gamma_n)\) are positive deterministic parameter gains. An Adam-type alternative fixes \(0\leq\alpha,\beta<1\) and \(\varepsilon>0\) and uses

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{n+1}&=\alpha M_n+(1-\alpha)H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1}),\\ V_{n+1}&=\beta V_n+(1-\beta)H_{\theta_n}(X_{n+1})^{\odot2},\\ \theta_{n+1}&=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1} \frac{M_{n+1}}{\sqrt{V_{n+1}}+\varepsilon}. \end{aligned} \tag{4.4}\]

Initially \(M_0\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and \(V_0\in[0,\infty)^d\). Squares, square roots and division are componentwise, and \(\varepsilon\) is added in each coordinate. We use uncorrected memories, without finite-time bias factors. The gains \(1-\alpha\) and \(1-\beta\) remain fixed. This is Equation 2.1 with memory \(U_n=(M_n,V_n)\) and readout \(G(x,m,v)=m/(\sqrt v+\varepsilon)\).

Fix \(\theta\) and set \(Y_k^\theta=H_\theta(X_k^\theta)\). Define the stationary memories at time zero by

\[ M^\theta=(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=0}^\infty\alpha^j Y_{-j}^\theta,\qquad V^\theta=(1-\beta)\sum_{j=0}^\infty\beta^j(Y_{-j}^\theta)^{\odot2}. \tag{4.5}\]

For a zero coefficient the corresponding series consists only of its \(j=0\) term. These variables are defined under \(\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}\).

Lemma 4.1 (Integrable stationary memories) Under Assumption 4.1, the series in Equation 4.5 converge absolutely almost surely and in \(L^1\). Moreover, \(M^\theta\in L^2\).

The proof is given in Appendix A.

The stationary Adam field is

\[ F(\theta) =\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}} \left[\frac{M^\theta}{\sqrt{V^\theta}+\varepsilon}\right]. \tag{4.6}\]

It is finite because \(\|M^\theta/(\sqrt{V^\theta}+\varepsilon)\|\leq\|M^\theta\|/\varepsilon\). This expectation is under the stationary frozen Markov path law, not the nonstationary law of Equation 4.2–Equation 4.4. For the IID kernel \(P_\theta(x,\cdot)=\pi_\theta(\cdot)\) it reduces to the history field of [3, Definition 1.3]. Neither the field’s existence nor the memory construction requires the additional restriction \(\alpha^2<\beta\) used in their ODE theorem.

4.3 Adam as a Markov augmentation

Fix \(\theta\). Let \(\mathsf Y=\mathbb{R}^d\times[0,\infty)^d\) be the memory space, with its Euclidean product norm, and let \(\mathsf Z=\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\) carry the product sigma-algebra. Define

\[ T_\theta(x',m,v)= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha m+(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x')\\ \beta v+(1-\beta)H_\theta(x')^{\odot2} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.7}\]

For \(u=(m,v)\), also write \(T_\theta(x,u)=T_\theta(x,m,v)\). For the frozen recursions, \(Z_n=(X_n,M_n,V_n)\) therefore has the kernel \(Q_\theta\) in Equation 2.2.

For two memory states driven by the same observation,

\[ \begin{aligned} \|T_\theta(x',m,v)-T_\theta(x',\widetilde m,\widetilde v)\|^2 &=\alpha^2\|m-\widetilde m\|^2+\beta^2\|v-\widetilde v\|^2\\ &\leq\kappa^2\|(m,v)-(\widetilde m,\widetilde v)\|^2, \qquad \kappa=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}<1. \end{aligned} \tag{4.8}\]

Thus the memory difference contracts along a common driving path, with coefficient \(\kappa=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}\). This estimate suffices to identify the invariant memory law under Assumption 4.1.

4.4 Identification of the stationary memories

Corollary 4.2 (Stationary memory representation) Under Assumption 4.1, \(Q_\theta\) has a unique invariant probability \(\nu_\theta\). Its driving marginal is \(\pi_\theta\) and

\[ \nu_\theta= \operatorname{Law}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}} (X_0^\theta,M^\theta,V^\theta), \tag{4.9}\]

where \(M^\theta,V^\theta\) are the stationary histories in Equation 4.5. Consequently,

\[ F(\theta)=\int_{\mathsf Z} \frac{m}{\sqrt v+\varepsilon}\, \nu_\theta(dx\,dm\,dv). \tag{4.10}\]

Proof. Define \(M_k^\theta\) and \(V_k^\theta\) by shifting the infinite series in Equation 4.5 to time \(k\). Their almost-sure convergence follows from Lemma 4.1 and stationarity. They satisfy the frozen recursions, while the joint process \((X_k^\theta,M_k^\theta,V_k^\theta)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\) is stationary. Its one-time law is therefore invariant for Equation 2.2.

For uniqueness, let \(\eta\) be another invariant probability of \(Q_\theta\). By the marginal identity in Chapter 2 and Assumption 4.1, its driving marginal equals \(\pi_\theta\). Couple initial states with laws \(\eta\) and \(\nu_\theta\) so that their driving coordinates agree, and then use the same driving transitions for both memory recursions. Their memory difference after \(n\) steps is bounded by \(\kappa^n\|U_0-\widetilde U_0\|\). This tends to zero almost surely without a moment assumption on the two initial memories. For every bounded measurable test function that is uniformly Lipschitz in the memory coordinate, bounded convergence gives equality of the two stationary expectations. Such tests determine probability measures on \(\mathsf X\times\mathsf Y\), hence \(\eta=\nu_\theta\). The integral representation follows from Lemma 4.1 and \(\varepsilon>0\).

The stationary representation follows from the invariant driver law, its innovation moment and synchronous memory contraction. Under the additional hypotheses of Theorem 3.1 and finite first moments in its metric, the invariant law constructed there is the same \(\nu_\theta\). In the affine EMA setting, the synchronous argument above gives uniqueness among all invariant probabilities.

4.5 Calibration under linear and nonlinear memory

Linear EMA momentum preserves the ordinary field. By the absolute integrability just proved, expectation and summation can be interchanged:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}[M^\theta] =(1-\alpha)\sum_{j\geq0}\alpha^j\mathbb{E}[Y_{-j}^\theta] =h(\theta). \tag{4.11}\]

No independence is used. Adam instead multiplies each momentum coordinate by \(W_i^\theta=(\sqrt{V_i^\theta}+\varepsilon)^{-1}\). Since \(0<W_i^\theta\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\) and \(M_i^\theta\in L^2\), the covariance below is well defined. Expanding its definition gives

\[ F_i(\theta)=h_i(\theta)\,\mathbb{E}[W_i^\theta] +\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}_\theta^{\mathrm{stat}}}(M_i^\theta,W_i^\theta). \tag{4.12}\]

Consequently, at an ordinary root,

\[ F_i(\theta^\star) =\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta^\star}^{\mathrm{stat}}} (M_i^{\theta^\star},W_i^{\theta^\star}). \tag{4.13}\]

Thus an ordinary root is also an Adam root exactly when these componentwise covariances vanish. The identity isolates the calibration issue: centring the innovation centres the first memory, but does not by itself centre the normalised readout. Determining the sign or size of the covariance, and the resulting root displacement, requires analysis of a particular model.

4.6 Verification of the memory and readout hypotheses

For Equation 4.7, use \(d_Y(u,v)=\|u-v\|\) and write

\[ T_\theta(x,u)=Au+b_\theta(x),\quad A=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha I_d,\beta I_d),\quad b_\theta(x)=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x)\\(1-\beta)H_\theta(x)^{\odot2}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.14}\]

If \(b_\theta\) is \(L_b\)-Lipschitz in \(d_X\), then Equation 3.3 holds with \(r=\max\{\alpha,\beta\}\) and \(L_T=L_b\). The one-step first-moment condition in Assumption 3.2 can be checked using Lemma 3.2 when its weight controls the metric. Spatial regularity of \(b_\theta\) requires checking the metric and innovation together, in addition to the stationary second-moment assumption.

4.6.1 Square-root second-moment coordinates

There is a distinction between regularity of the memory update and regularity of its readout. The map \((m,v)\mapsto m/(\sqrt v+\varepsilon)\) is not globally Lipschitz in \(v\) under the ordinary Euclidean metric near \(v=0\). For a bounded innovation, however, a change of memory coordinates removes this difficulty without modifying the Adam recursion.

Assume, for the remainder of this subsection, that

\[ \|H_\theta(x)\|_\infty\leq B_H,\qquad \|H_\theta(x)-H_\theta(y)\|\leq L_Hd_X(x,y). \tag{4.15}\]

Put \(S_n=\sqrt{V_n}\) coordinatewise and use the forward-invariant memory set

\[ \mathsf Y_B=\{(m,s):\|m\|_\infty\leq B_H,\quad 0\leq s_i\leq B_H\}, \]

with metric \(d_Y((m,s),(m',s'))=\|m-m'\|+\|s-s'\|\). With initial memories \((M_0,S_0)\in\mathsf Y_B\), the update is

\[ \widehat T_\theta(x,m,s)= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha m+(1-\alpha)H_\theta(x)\\ \sqrt{\beta s^{\odot2}+(1-\beta)H_\theta(x)^{\odot2}} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.16}\]

For each coordinate, the second component is the Euclidean norm of \((\sqrt\beta\,s_i,\sqrt{1-\beta}\,H_{\theta,i}(x))\). The reverse triangle inequality therefore gives

\[ \begin{aligned} d_Y(\widehat T_\theta(x,u),\widehat T_\theta(y,v)) \leq {}&\max\{\alpha,\sqrt\beta\}\,d_Y(u,v)\\ &+\bigl(1-\alpha+\sqrt{1-\beta}\bigr)L_Hd_X(x,y). \end{aligned} \tag{4.17}\]

Thus the transformed memory satisfies Equation 3.3, with contraction coefficient \(\max\{\alpha,\sqrt\beta\}<1\). On \(\mathsf Y_B\) the Adam readout

\[ G(m,s)=\frac{m}{s+\varepsilon} \tag{4.18}\]

is Lipschitz:

\[ \|G(m,s)-G(m',s')\| \leq\varepsilon^{-1}\|m-m'\| +B_H\varepsilon^{-2}\|s-s'\|. \tag{4.19}\]

These coordinates represent the same finite-dimensional memory process. Since the transformed memory is bounded, a driver one-step first moment supplies the remaining moment condition in Assumption 3.2. It gives a direct Lipschitz observable for the Poisson equation when the innovation is bounded, as for an acceptance-rate residual. For unbounded innovations the affine \((m,v)\) representation still admits Theorem 3.1 under its stated assumptions; the Lipschitz property of the Adam readout must then be addressed separately.

5 Parameter perturbation and Poisson regularity

The contraction theorem is stated for each frozen parameter, with constants uniform on a specified region. We now compare two such frozen kernels, \(Q_\theta\) and \(Q_\vartheta\). The comparison is an operator calculation on Lipschitz test functions; it does not require constructing an additional coupling of the augmented transitions.

5.1 One-step kernel regularity

For \(f:\mathsf Z\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(z=(x,u)\), write

\[ f_{\theta,u}(y)=f(y,T_\theta(y,u)). \tag{5.1}\]

By Equation 2.2,

\[ Q_\theta f(x,u)=P_\theta f_{\theta,u}(x). \]

Adding and subtracting \(P_\vartheta f_{\theta,u}(x)\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} (Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f(x,u) ={}&(P_\theta-P_\vartheta)f_{\theta,u}(x)\\ &+P_\vartheta(f_{\theta,u}-f_{\vartheta,u})(x). \end{aligned} \tag{5.2}\]

Under Assumption 3.2, \(\|f_{\theta,u}\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_X)}\leq(1+L_T)\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}\), and

\[ |f_{\theta,u}(y)-f_{\vartheta,u}(y)| \leq\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)} d_Y(T_\theta(y,u),T_\vartheta(y,u)). \]

Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality therefore yields

\[ \begin{aligned} &W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta((x,u),\cdot),Q_\vartheta((x,u),\cdot))\\ &\quad\leq (1+L_T)W_{d_X}(P_\theta(x,\cdot),P_\vartheta(x,\cdot))\\ &\qquad+P_\vartheta\!\left[ d_Y(T_\theta(\cdot,u),T_\vartheta(\cdot,u)) \right](x). \end{aligned} \tag{5.3}\]

The first term measures the change in the driving kernel; the second measures the change in the memory update under the same driving law. For the affine EMA, the second term depends only on \(b_\theta-b_\vartheta\), since \(A\) is fixed. In the square-root coordinates of Equation 4.16 it can be controlled instead by the parameter difference of the original innovation \(H_\theta\).

Assumption 5.1 (Driver-level parameter regularity) On a parameter region \(\mathsf K\), assume there is a measurable \(w:\mathsf X\to[1,\infty)\) and constants \(L_P,L_{T,\theta}<\infty\), uniform over \(\theta,\vartheta\in\mathsf K\), such that

\[ \begin{aligned} W_{d_X}(P_\theta(x,\cdot),P_\vartheta(x,\cdot)) &\leq L_P\|\theta-\vartheta\|w(x),\\ P_\vartheta[d_Y(T_\theta(\cdot,u),T_\vartheta(\cdot,u))](x) &\leq L_{T,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|w(x) \end{aligned} \tag{5.4}\]

for all \(x\) and \(u\) in the memory space under consideration. Let \(\mathcal V:\mathsf Z\to[1,\infty)\) dominate \(w(x)\) and suppose the family \((Q_\theta)_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\) has uniform geometric Wasserstein contraction as in Theorem 3.1.

The bounds in Assumption 5.1 are verification assumptions on the driver and forcing, not consequences of frozen contraction.

Proposition 5.1 (One-step regularity of augmented kernels) Under Assumption 3.2 and Assumption 5.1,

\[ \gamma_{\theta,\vartheta} :=\sup_{z\in\mathsf Z} \frac{W_{d_Z}(Q_\theta(z,\cdot),Q_\vartheta(z,\cdot))} {\mathcal V(z)} \leq L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|, \tag{5.5}\]

with \(L_Q=(1+L_T)L_P+L_{T,\theta}\). Equivalently, for every \(d_Z\)-Lipschitz test \(f\),

\[ |(Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)f(z)| \leq L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|\mathcal V(z) \|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}. \tag{5.6}\]

Proof. Apply Equation 5.3, substitute Equation 5.4 and divide by \(\mathcal V(z)\geq w(x)\). The equivalent operator estimate is the dual representation of \(W_{d_Z}\).

5.2 Perturbation of invariant laws

The geometric estimate of Theorem 3.1 and the weighted one-step bound enter the Wasserstein perturbation framework of [4, Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.1]. The stationary estimate below uses the invariant weighted moment directly; a Lyapunov inequality is one way to obtain that moment. Its proof retains the operator calculation needed for Poisson regularity.

Corollary 5.2 (Lipschitz dependence of augmented invariant laws) Suppose \(Q_\theta\) has invariant law \(\nu_\theta\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\), satisfies Equation 3.4 and obeys Equation 5.6, as supplied by Proposition 5.1. Suppose also that

\[ \sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)\leq M_{\mathcal V}<\infty. \tag{5.7}\]

Then for every \(\theta,\vartheta\in\mathsf K\),

\[ W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta) \leq \frac{C_Z(\eta)L_QM_{\mathcal V}}{1-\eta} \|\theta-\vartheta\|. \tag{5.8}\]

Proof. Fix a \(1\)-Lipschitz \(f\). Since \(\nu_\theta\) and \(\nu_\vartheta\) are invariant,

\[ \begin{aligned} |\nu_\theta(f)-\nu_\vartheta(f)| \leq{}& |(\nu_\theta-\nu_\vartheta)(Q_\theta^n f)|\\ &+|\nu_\vartheta(Q_\theta^nf-Q_\vartheta^nf)|. \end{aligned} \]

The first term is at most \(C_Z(\eta)\eta^n W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta)\). For the second use

\[ Q_\theta^n-Q_\vartheta^n =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}Q_\vartheta^j (Q_\theta-Q_\vartheta)Q_\theta^{n-1-j}. \tag{5.9}\]

The stationarity of \(\nu_\vartheta\), Equation 5.6 and the bound \(\|Q_\theta^k f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq C_Z(\eta)\eta^k\) give

\[ |\nu_\vartheta(Q_\theta^nf-Q_\vartheta^nf)| \leq C_Z(\eta)L_Q\|\theta-\vartheta\|\, \nu_\vartheta(\mathcal V)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\eta^{n-1-j}. \]

Let \(n\to\infty\). The first term tends to zero, and the geometric sum is bounded by \((1-\eta)^{-1}\). Taking the supremum over \(f\) proves Equation 5.8.

Uniform drift constants in Equation 3.9, together with suitable moment conditions, provide one route to Equation 5.7. The constants in Equation 5.8 depend on the contraction, moment and perturbation bounds on \(\mathsf K\).

5.3 Regularity of the stationary mean field

Suppose \(G_\theta:\mathsf Z\to\mathbb{R}^d\) has a uniform Lipschitz seminorm and satisfies

\[ \|G_\theta(z)-G_\vartheta(z)\| \leq L_{G,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|\mathcal V(z). \tag{5.10}\]

For \(F(\theta)=\nu_\theta(G_\theta)\), add and subtract \(\nu_\vartheta(G_\theta)\) to obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} \|F(\theta)-F(\vartheta)\| \leq{}& \|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\, W_{d_Z}(\nu_\theta,\nu_\vartheta)\\ &+\nu_\vartheta(\|G_\theta-G_\vartheta\|). \end{aligned} \tag{5.11}\]

Thus Corollary 5.2 gives local Lipschitz regularity of \(F\) under Equation 5.10. For the square-root memory coordinates of Equation 4.18 the readout is independent of \(\theta\), so the second term vanishes. This Lipschitz bound will also supply the regularity of the limiting ODE field.

5.4 Eccentricity and weighted growth

Write \(E_{1,\theta}(z)=\int d_Z(z,z')\,\nu_\theta(dz')\) for the first eccentricity. Suppose \(E_{1,\theta}(z)\leq C_E\mathcal V(z)\) uniformly for \(\theta\in\mathsf K\). Centring a Lipschitz function under \(\nu_\theta\) gives

\[ \|f-\nu_\theta(f)\|_{\mathcal V} \leq C_E\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}. \tag{5.12}\]

Indeed, \(|f(z)-\nu_\theta(f)|\leq\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}E_{1,\theta}(z)\). The triangle inequality also gives

\[ E_{1,\theta}(z) \leq d_Z(z,z_0)+\nu_\theta(d_Z(z_0,\cdot)). \]

Consequently the comparison is uniform if \(d_Z(z,z_0)\leq c\mathcal V(z)\) and \(\sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)<\infty\). For a zero-mass signed measure \(\xi\) with finite weighted variation, define \(\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{KR}}=\sup_{\|f\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq1}|\xi(f)|\). Subtracting \(\nu_\theta(f)\) inside \(\xi(f)\) and using the same embedding gives

\[ \|\xi\|_{\mathrm{KR}} \leq C_E\int\mathcal V(z)\,|\xi|(dz). \tag{5.13}\]

Eccentricity thus links the Lipschitz estimates obtained from Wasserstein contraction to the weighted-growth estimates used in [7].

5.5 Frozen Poisson solution

For a scalar Lipschitz observable \(G_\theta\), set \(\bar G_\theta=G_\theta-F(\theta)\) as in Equation 2.13; vector-valued observables are treated coordinatewise. Geometric Wasserstein contraction and finite first eccentricity give the unique centred Lipschitz solution [5, Theorem 3.4], represented by Equation 2.14, with \(\nu_\theta(u_\theta)=0\) and

\[ \|u_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)} \leq\Lambda_\theta\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}(d_Z)}, \qquad \Lambda_\theta:=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n) \leq\frac{C_Z(\eta)}{1-\eta}. \tag{5.14}\]

To see the summability directly, Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality gives

\[ |Q_\theta^nG_\theta(z)-F(\theta)| \leq \tau_{d_Z}(Q_\theta^n) \|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}E_{1,\theta}(z). \tag{5.15}\]

The right-hand side is summable in \(n\). It is also integrable under \(\nu_\theta\), since \(\nu_\theta\in\mathcal P_1\). Thus termwise integration centres the solution, and shifting the series proves Equation 2.13. If two centred Lipschitz solutions existed, their difference \(v\) would satisfy \(v=Q_\theta^n v\) for every \(n\); contraction forces its Lipschitz seminorm to vanish, and centring then gives \(v=0\).

Applying the Neumann-series bound yields

\[ \|u_\theta\|_{\mathcal V} \leq C_E\Lambda_\theta\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}. \tag{5.16}\]

The pointwise estimate \(|u_\theta(z)|\leq\Lambda_\theta\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}E_{1,\theta}(z)\) also gives stationary \(L^p\) bounds whenever \(\nu_\theta(E_{1,\theta}^p)<\infty\) [5, Theorem 3.5]. For bounded innovations, Equation 4.19 verifies the Lipschitz readout hypothesis for Adam.

5.6 Parameter regularity of the Poisson solution

The weighted one-step perturbation bound of Proposition 5.1 combines with the decay of centred iterates to give a parameter modulus for the Poisson solution. This follows the comparison-of-iterates approach of [7, Theorem 2], using Wasserstein contraction on the augmented space.

Proposition 5.3 (Log-Lipschitz parameter regularity) Let \(\mathsf K\) be a compact parameter set. Assume uniformly over \(\theta,\vartheta\in\mathsf K\) that:

  1. \(Q_\theta\) satisfies Equation 3.4 with common constants \(C_Z<\infty\) and \(\rho\in(0,1)\), and has invariant law \(\nu_\theta\in\mathcal P_1(\mathsf Z)\).
  2. The one-step weighted estimate Equation 5.6 holds, and \(Q_\theta\mathcal V\leq a\mathcal V+b\) for some \(0\leq a<1\), \(b<\infty\).
  3. \(\sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)<\infty\) and \(d_Z(z,z_0)\leq c\mathcal V(z)\) for some \(z_0\).
  4. \(\sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K}\|G_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}}\leq L_G\) and \(|G_\theta(z)-G_\vartheta(z)|\leq L_{G,\theta}\|\theta-\vartheta\|\mathcal V(z)\).

Assume the kernels and observables are jointly measurable in parameter and state. For \(t>0\), set \(\omega(t)=t(1+\log^+(1/t))\) and \(\omega(0)=0\). Then there is \(C<\infty\) such that

\[ |u_\theta(z)-u_\vartheta(z)| \leq C\mathcal V(z)\omega(\|\theta-\vartheta\|). \tag{5.17}\]

The same estimate holds for \(\phi_\theta=Q_\theta u_\theta\):

\[ |\phi_\theta(z)-\phi_\vartheta(z)| \leq C\mathcal V(z)\omega(\|\theta-\vartheta\|). \tag{5.18}\]

Proof. Put \(\delta=\|\theta-\vartheta\|\) and write

\[ R_\theta^nG_\theta(z)=Q_\theta^nG_\theta(z)-F(\theta), \qquad D_n(z)=R_\theta^nG_\theta(z)-R_\vartheta^nG_\vartheta(z). \]

The drift assumption gives \(\sup_{\theta,n}Q_\theta^n\mathcal V(z)\leq C_V\mathcal V(z)\). By the eccentricity comparison and Equation 3.5,

\[ |R_\theta^nG_\theta(z)| \leq C_Z\rho^n L_GE_{1,\theta}(z) \leq C_1\rho^n\mathcal V(z), \]

so \(|D_n(z)|\leq 2C_1\rho^n\mathcal V(z)\).

For a second bound, decompose

\[ \begin{aligned} D_n={}&(Q_\theta^n-Q_\vartheta^n)G_\theta\\ &+Q_\vartheta^n(G_\theta-G_\vartheta) -\bigl(F(\theta)-F(\vartheta)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

The last term is \(O(\delta)\) by Equation 5.11 and Corollary 5.2. The middle term is at most \(L_{G,\theta}C_V\delta\mathcal V(z)\). For the first term, telescope as in Equation 5.9. Wasserstein contraction gives

\[ \|Q_\theta^kG_\theta\|_{\operatorname{Lip}} \leq C_Z\rho^kL_G. \]

Applying Equation 5.6 inside the telescoping sum,

\[ \begin{aligned} &|(Q_\theta^n-Q_\vartheta^n)G_\theta(z)|\\ &\quad\leq C_ZL_QL_G\delta \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\rho^{n-1-j}Q_\vartheta^j\mathcal V(z)\\ &\quad\leq \frac{C_ZL_QL_GC_V}{1-\rho}\, \delta\mathcal V(z). \end{aligned} \tag{5.19}\]

Thus \(|D_n(z)|\leq C_2\delta\mathcal V(z)\), uniformly in \(n\). Combining the two estimates and summing the Neumann series yields

\[ \begin{aligned} |u_\theta(z)-u_\vartheta(z)| &\leq C_3\mathcal V(z) \sum_{n=0}^\infty\min\{\delta,\rho^n\}\\ &\leq C_4\mathcal V(z)\omega(\delta). \end{aligned} \]

For \(0<\delta<1\), choose \(N=\lceil\log(1/\delta)/|\log\rho|\rceil\). The first \(N\) terms contribute at most \(N\delta\) and the remaining terms at most \(\rho^N/(1-\rho)\leq\delta/(1-\rho)\). For \(\delta\geq1\) the geometric bound suffices, and \(\delta=0\) is immediate. Finally, the Poisson identity gives \(\phi_\theta=u_\theta-G_\theta+F(\theta)\). The parameter estimates for its three terms prove Equation 5.18.

The parameter modulus follows from the weighted one-step perturbation bound Equation 5.6. Its logarithmic term measures the number of iterations required for geometric forgetting to reach the scale of a parameter change.

6 ODE tracking and adaptive MCMC

We return to the changing-parameter recursion Equation 2.1. The Poisson solution constructed above separates its centred observation noise into a martingale difference and telescoping remainders. This is the stochastic approximation approach of [8, 9], applied to the augmented state.

6.1 Poisson martingale decomposition

In augmented coordinates the adaptive recursion is

\[ Z_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n\sim Q_{\theta_n}(Z_n,\cdot), \qquad \theta_{n+1}=\theta_n+\gamma_{n+1}G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1}). \tag{6.1}\]

Define

\[ \Delta M_{n+1} =u_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-Q_{\theta_n}u_{\theta_n}(Z_n), \qquad \phi_\theta=Q_\theta u_\theta. \tag{6.2}\]

The Poisson equation gives

\[ G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-F(\theta_n) =\Delta M_{n+1} +\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)-\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1}). \tag{6.3}\]

When \(u_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})\) is square-integrable, the conditional mean of \(\Delta M_{n+1}\) is zero. For scalar observables, its conditional variance satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[(\Delta M_{n+1})^2\mid\mathcal F_n]\\ &\qquad=Q_{\theta_n}u_{\theta_n}^2(Z_n) -\bigl(Q_{\theta_n}u_{\theta_n}(Z_n)\bigr)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{6.4}\]

This is the martingale calculation in [6, Lemma 4], applied here to \(Q_\theta\) on the augmented space. For vector-valued updates the corresponding identity holds with squared Euclidean norms. The weighted Poisson bound and a second-order drift estimate control its expectation along the adaptive path.

6.2 Adaptive moment control and tracking

Theorem 6.1 (ODE tracking under Markov noise with contractive memory) Assume Proposition 5.3 and suppose that, uniformly for \(\theta\in\mathsf K\),

\[ Q_\theta\mathcal V^2\leq a_2\mathcal V^2+b_2, \qquad 0\leq a_2<1,\quad b_2<\infty. \tag{6.5}\]

Let \(\mathbb E[\mathcal V^2(Z_0)]<\infty\), and suppose the unprojected recursion Equation 6.1 remains in \(\mathsf K\) almost surely. Assume also that \(\sup_{\theta\in\mathsf K,z\in\mathsf Z}|G_\theta(z)|\leq B<\infty\). Let \((\gamma_n)\) be deterministic, positive and nonincreasing, with

\[ \gamma_n\to0,\qquad \sum_n\gamma_n=\infty,\qquad \sum_n\gamma_n^2(1+|\log\gamma_n|)<\infty. \tag{6.6}\]

Then

\[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty\gamma_{n+1} \bigl[G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-F(\theta_n)\bigr] \quad\text{converges almost surely}. \tag{6.7}\]

If \(F\) has a Lipschitz extension to a neighbourhood carrying its forward ODE flow \(\Phi\), the piecewise-linear interpolation \(\bar\theta\) on the timescale \(t_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\gamma_k\) is an asymptotic pseudo-trajectory of

\[ \dot\theta=F(\theta) \tag{6.8}\]

(using that extension for the flow). In particular, for every \(T>0\),

\[ \lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq s\leq T} |\bar\theta(t+s)-\Phi_s(\bar\theta(t))|=0 \qquad\text{almost surely}. \tag{6.9}\]

Proof. Iterating Equation 6.5 conditionally shows \(\sup_n\mathbb E[\mathcal V^2(Z_n)]<\infty\). The frozen Poisson and drift estimates give \(|u_\theta|+|\phi_\theta|\leq C\mathcal V\). Using Equation 6.4,

\[ \sum_n\gamma_{n+1}^2 \mathbb E[|\Delta M_{n+1}|^2] \leq C\sum_n\gamma_{n+1}^2<\infty, \]

so the weighted martingale converges almost surely.

For the remaining terms of Equation 6.3, summation by parts yields, for \(N\geq1\),

\[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\gamma_{n+1} \bigl[G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-F(\theta_n)\bigr]\\ ={}&\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\gamma_{n+1}\Delta M_{n+1} +\gamma_1\phi_{\theta_0}(Z_0) -\gamma_N\phi_{\theta_{N-1}}(Z_N)\\ &+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_n)\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)\\ &+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\gamma_n \bigl[\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)-\phi_{\theta_{n-1}}(Z_n)\bigr]. \end{aligned} \tag{6.10}\]

The terminal boundary term tends to zero almost surely, since its squared expectation is bounded by \(C\gamma_N^2\) and \(\sum_N\gamma_N^2<\infty\). The gain-variation series is absolutely summable almost surely because \(\sum_n|\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_n|<\infty\) and \(\sup_n\mathbb E[\mathcal V(Z_n)]<\infty\).

Finally, \(|\theta_n-\theta_{n-1}|\leq B\gamma_n\), and Equation 5.18 gives, for all sufficiently large \(n\),

\[ \gamma_n|\phi_{\theta_n}(Z_n)-\phi_{\theta_{n-1}}(Z_n)| \leq C\gamma_n^2(1+|\log\gamma_n|)\mathcal V(Z_n). \tag{6.11}\]

The right-hand side is summable almost surely by Equation 6.6 and the uniform first-moment bound. Thus the weighted centred-noise series converges. Put \(\epsilon_{n+1}=G_{\theta_n}(Z_{n+1})-F(\theta_n)\). Convergence of the weighted noise series gives

\[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{m\geq n} \left\|\sum_{k=n}^{m}\gamma_{k+1}\epsilon_{k+1}\right\|=0 \qquad\text{almost surely}. \]

The bounded readout also gives \(\|F(\theta)\|\leq B\) on \(\mathsf K\) and \(\|\epsilon_{n+1}\|\leq2B\). Thus fractional mesh intervals contribute at most \(2B\gamma_{n+1}\) to the accumulated noise. On each interval \([t_n,t_{n+1}]\), \(\|\bar\theta(t)-\theta_n\|\leq B\gamma_{n+1}\). On any fixed time horizon, the Lipschitz extension of \(F\) therefore makes the accumulated Euler discretisation error tend to zero. Comparing the resulting integral equation with the ODE by Gronwall’s inequality proves Equation 6.9, first at mesh points and then at arbitrary starting times.

6.3 Adam adaptation in MCMC

For the bounded-innovation Adam representation in Equation 4.16, Equation 4.19 gives the Lipschitz readout estimate and \(\|G\|\leq\sqrt d B_H/\varepsilon\). The forward-invariant set \(\mathsf Y_B\) therefore supplies the bounded parameter increments required above. The stationary construction in Corollary 4.2 identifies its invariant field with Equation 4.6: the square-root coordinates simply push forward the same memory law. Under the driver coupling, weighted parameter perturbation, quadratic drift, stability and flow assumptions, Theorem 6.1 then gives

\[ \dot\theta=F(\theta) =\mathbb E_{\nu_\theta} \left[\frac{M}{\sqrt V+\varepsilon}\right]. \tag{6.12}\]

For acceptance-rate calibration, the driving state includes the proposal or acceptance variables whenever they enter the observed residual. The coupling and parameter estimates must hold for that driver on the chosen parameter localisation set. Quadratic drift controls moments along the adaptive path; frozen stationary moments alone do not supply that control. The theorem assumes stability of the unprojected parameter recursion, so a localisation argument must establish the same conditions on the region where the recursion is analysed. Projected recursions require the corresponding projected ODE analysis.

These conditions leave the calibration distinction in Equation 4.13 intact. The limiting field is \(F\); identifying its equilibria and their attraction properties is a further model-specific question.

7 Discussion

The Markov augmentation represents the memories by a finite-dimensional transition kernel. Under the driver-coupling and forcing assumptions of Theorem 3.1, the augmented kernel is geometrically contractive in Wasserstein distance. This permits invariant-law perturbation estimates [4] and the frozen Poisson construction of [5]. Eccentricity relates the resulting Lipschitz estimates to the weighted-growth setting used by [7].

The parameter-dependent Poisson result Proposition 5.3 combines geometric decay with the weighted one-step perturbation bound. The resulting log-Lipschitz modulus is sufficient to control the adaptive feedback term for gains satisfying Equation 6.6. Together with a quadratic Lyapunov drift and parameter stability, Theorem 6.1 establishes tracking of the stationary field \(\dot\theta=F(\theta)\).

The stationary Adam field averages a nonlinear readout of joint first- and second-moment memories. Linear momentum preserves \(h\), while Equation 4.12 identifies the covariance correction introduced by Adam’s random normaliser. This correction depends on the joint stationary memory law and can therefore reflect temporal dependence in the Markov innovations.

For bounded acceptance-rate innovations, the square-root second-moment coordinate provides a bounded Lipschitz Adam readout. The main application-specific work is to verify the driver coupling, parameter perturbation, quadratic drift and stability assumptions for a chosen MCMC sampler. A numerical example could then quantify the stationary covariance correction and its effect on calibration.

The Markovian iterated-function-system results of [10] also suggest a direction for weakening the geometric coupling assumptions through contraction on average. Such extensions would broaden the range of drivers covered by the augmented analysis.

This proves Lemma 3.2. With the notation of Equation 3.7–Equation 3.8,

Proof. The growth estimate in Equation 3.7 gives \(Q_\theta\mathcal V(x,u)\leq1+(k+c_1)P_\theta w(x)+r\|u\|+c_0\). Substituting the driver drift,

\[ \begin{aligned} Q_\theta\mathcal V(x,u) &\leq 1+(k+c_1)(aw(x)+B)+r\|u\|+c_0\\ &\leq\delta\mathcal V(x,u) +1-\delta+(k+c_1)B+c_0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(L=1-\delta+(k+c_1)B+c_0\) is a uniform drift constant when the driver and forcing constants are uniform. For an invariant law with finite \(\mathcal V\)-moment, integrating gives \((1-\delta)\nu_\theta(\mathcal V)\leq L\).
